Same Song, Louder Dance

Why AI literacy is judgment, not prompt magic

Artificial intelligence has always been molded to fit whatever a business wanted to sell.

It is a familiar song. Companies claim their application has AI. They promise brand-new machine learning algorithms. They say this time is different. In my childhood, that often meant selling linear regression as God’s greatest gift, as folks realized we could make predictions about future events based on data from past events.

Of course, the engineers yelled from the background: “That isn’t real intelligence. That is just math!”

Twenty years changes a lot.

Unless you have been living in a cave, new AI models built around large language models have taken off. Nowadays, companies once again claim their applications have the best AI and the newest machine learning algorithms. You have CEOs like Dario Amodei of Anthropic and Sam Altman of OpenAI making sweeping claims about artificial general intelligence, work as we know it, universal basic income, and the final industrial revolution.

Same song. Same dance. Just louder.

On one side, the CEOs claim we are on the path toward general intelligence. On the other side, engineers are still yelling, “It is just math!” Then the mathematicians yell back, “Yes, but it is language!”

The truth, of course, is somewhere in the middle.

Gradient descent packaged with massive amounts of unstructured text turned out to be very powerful. While large language models feel different from the regression models and older machine learning tools many of us learned first, they still come from the same broader tradition: using mathematical methods to find patterns in data. The methods have changed. The scale has changed. The usefulness has changed. But the temptation to oversell it has not.

It would be a mistake to fully ignore this technology as a marketing ploy. It would also be a mistake to fully buy into the marketing message. This is a powerful, fast-moving technology that will have a tangible impact. AI literacy is going to be a necessary skill for navigating the future. Not just in the workforce, but in everyday life.

I have always been interested in intelligence. Human intelligence, animal intelligence, machine intelligence. How do we learn? How do we adapt? How do we solve problems? Professionally, I get to work on a few of these questions, mostly around how humans learn and how math and traditional machine learning methods can be applied in useful ways.

Some of the first academic papers I read were centered on machine learning. I learned by reimplementing old papers from the 1980s around the development of early checkers-playing AI. Similar ideas would later be used to develop AI for more complex games like Go and chess. My undergraduate research project was building a neural network from scratch to play Mū Tōrere, an ancient New Zealand board game.

So I have never really been in the “it is all fake” camp. I have also never been fully in the “this changes everything tomorrow morning” camp.

Twenty years ago, the Turing test was still treated as one of the defining lines for when we would enter a new age of AI. There had been chatbots before, even when I was a kid, but they were usually simple and brittle. Even by 2018, when I transitioned from technology into education, the closest many people had seen to a sophisticated chatbot was Microsoft’s infamous Twitter bot, Tay.

Tay either had been programmed to repeat people or learned to repeat people, depending on how generously you want to tell the story. Either way, the internet quickly weaponized her, and within 16 hours she had been taken down and lobotomized.

In my first couple years working with high school students, I ran my own little Turing test as part of an activity on neural networks and AI. Each class would ask 20 questions. I would feed half of the questions to AI and half to friends or family. Then I would bring the answers back and have students guess which responses came from a human and which came from a machine.

In 2018 and 2019, the bots were still rough. I would sometimes make a few slight edits to the AI responses to make them less obviously robotic, tipping the scale a bit. Then students would analyze the results.

What surprised me was that even before modern LLMs, only a very small handful of students could predict with any real confidence whether something was written by AI or by a human.

I felt comfortable tipping the scales a little in favor of the AI because I knew improvement usually is not linear. Bots may not have seemed to change much in the previous ten years, but I had confidence that behind the scenes things were improving. Still, even I could not have predicted how close, how simple, and how impactful large language models would become in my lifetime.

The velocity of adoption feels like the internet after the dot-com bust, when the hype had cooled but the actual use exploded. I thought maybe by the end of my life we would have a bot that could convincingly pass a Turing test. Now the debate is not whether AI can imitate a human in a conversation. The debate is how general this intelligence is, what tasks it can do, and what tasks we are still confident it cannot.

That is why AI literacy matters.

Not because every claim from an AI company is true. Not because every model is secretly intelligent in the same way humans are. And not because “it is just math” is wrong, exactly. It is math. But sometimes math, scaled up and pointed at the right kind of data, becomes a tool powerful enough to reshape how people learn, work, write, search, and make decisions.

The future will not belong to people who blindly trust AI. It also will not belong to people who reflexively dismiss it.

It will belong to people who understand enough to ask better questions.

“Come sit down, we’re lamenting / About yesterday’s sad ending.”
— Erik Petersen, “Olde Tyme Mem’ry”

There will be a day when handwritten code is seen as old school, like using some specific wood carving tool instead of a power saw. Maybe that day has already come and passed.

As an early adopter of Cursor, I have already had my identity crisis. I have watched these tools code as well as most programmers I know, including myself. We have spent the last few years in a reality where almost any computer science assignment from my bachelor’s or master’s degree could be done by these machines. Now, with tools like Codex, Antigravity, Claude Code, and Cursor, these AI systems are not just sitting in a browser window. They are integrated directly into our IDEs and projects.

The harnesses have improved at a similar pace as the models themselves. They have changed alongside them. And while I have fallen in love with my current tool, it was not love at first sight.

Unlike most programming professionals I have met in the Midwest, I am a true believer in coding itself. I cut my teeth writing Visual Basic 6 programs when I was 12, then graduated to C++ and SDL2. I have always spent some chunk of my week reading code, writing programs, or poking at some weird little project regardless of what was happening in my personal or professional life.

Like writing, I see programming as a way of thinking through a problem. The process of programming has made me better at general problem solving. It is, and will always be, a passion of mine.

But in this world, you need to be open to change.

No one has taken programming from me. I could still spend my weekends coding by hand, thinking through each line and variable. Occasionally, I do. Sometimes I want to use my hands to build something solid and steady. Something that feels like it might stand the test of time. Sometimes I want to descend into old-time memory and pretend the days of wooden wood are still with us.

Most of my time, though, is spent building.

Another passion of mine has always been weird projects. I go through stretches where I fill my idea document with projects I will never have enough time to finish. I would love to have a website where I can connect my Spotify and practice guessing the beats per minute of songs to better train my ear for DJing. I would love an app that visualizes open-source data in Kansas City. I would love to know where the most dangerous intersection in Kansas City is. Board members for every organization in KC are public, so wouldn’t it be cool to have a map of everyone in Kansas City and how all those boards are connected?

And it goes on.

And on.

And on.

I will not lie. For about a year there, it became almost too easy to start answering those questions. Too easy to spin up those projects. I would jump from project to project, and from the outside it probably looked like I was not getting much done. In some ways, that was fair. I built a lot of those things, but building something is not the same as releasing it, maintaining it, documenting it, and keeping it alive after the fun part is over.

That has been one of the lessons of these tools. The old way of building does not quite fit the new paradigm when a week’s worth of handmade code can be done in a day. Architecture and design matter more, not less. Since you are not slowly building every board by hand, you need to make sure the foundation is solid before you start stacking floors on top of it.

The process matters more than the harness itself.

For the most part, these tools work in similar ways. Some are integrated directly into VS Code or a VS Code-like environment, like Copilot, Cursor, and optionally Antigravity. Others, like Codex and Claude Code, are more separate, which means you can use whatever IDE you prefer. I tend to like the integrated approach because it is easier to review the code. You can see the changes, approve or reject them in smaller batches, and keep the feedback loop closer to the actual work.

Most of these tools also let you select between multiple models, even when they are still pushing you toward a particular vendor. That model choice matters, because part of the new workflow is managing usage. Intelligence is being rationed now, which is not a sentence I love typing, but here we are.

For a while, Silicon Valley seemed to be in its tokenmaxxing phase. Now AI usage is getting more expensive, or at least more constrained, which means developers have to think about where the expensive model is actually worth it. Maybe you use the best model, with limited weekly usage, for planning and architecture. Then you use a cheaper, faster, stupider model for the actual coding passes. I have found that to be pretty effective. For a while, I would use the best Claude model for planning and something like Gemini for the coding.

That is part of why my tool choices changed.

I was an early adopter of Cursor shortly after they pivoted to AI in 2023. The subsidization of AI at the time meant they could offer what felt like unlimited usage for $20 a month. I probably burned through thousands of dollars of model usage for that $20, both professionally and personally. I would build all day and night.

I ended up switching at the start of the year when Cursor started charging for every query. I was willing to pay more. Honestly, I expected to pay more. But I wanted an unlimited plan, or at least something that did not make every question feel like I was dropping a coin into a machine.

I evaluated Claude Code because I was using Claude’s models so much at the time. But Google’s Antigravity had a very generous paid tier that came with the same amount of Claude credits I wanted anyway.

Then I actually used it.

Antigravity was integrated into VS Code and had a unique feature where it could run the application’s frontend without human input. I have written my share of Eggplant scripts, so seeing that in action was surreal. It would go through the app, test and run the frontend, do the kinds of things a user would do, and feed that back into the model.

The part that still sticks with me is watching it test the frontend on the same machine while I was playing video games. It was clicking through the app, seeing what happened, adjusting its next step, and continuing the loop while I was sitting there half-working and half-playing.

Some part of me wanted to call that fake.

The part of me that has written those scripts knew exactly how real it was.

Of course, Google has had some missteps since then. They lowered the quota of Claude credits after downstream changes with Anthropic, which was annoying. But they also lowered the price. They have also taken steps to improve their own models quickly. Before, I used Claude for almost all planning and preferred it for coding. Since Gemini 3.5 Flash, I have mostly used that model for almost everything.

I am happy to pay for the tier I have. Based on my usage, I do not get close to the Gemini quota and only occasionally hit the Claude one. That is enough for now.

But I have learned not to get too attached.

It is not the harness itself. It is the price, the model mix, and the process. Plus, there may be reasons not to engage with the company itself. Google is at least the tech evil I know. Cursor’s recent acquisition by SpaceX means it is now owned by Elon Musk, which means the writing is on the wall one way or another. Technically Twitter still runs, but saying it is the same product is comparing apples and shit.

I also know I am not the only programmer using these tools professionally. This is not just a Silicon Valley trend that will disappear when the vibes shift. Even in 2023 and early 2024, when I interviewed at a couple places, every team had developers using these tools. I support interns at a variety of organizations in the area, and they are now expected to use AI tools alongside their work.

I have given these tools to my interns because I need them to understand how to use them ethically. It has allowed them to understand codebases that would have taken them months to make sense of before. It has also helped them get actual work done.

That does not mean I think the trade-off is simple.

They are learning a different abstraction. Is that better than the old way? I do not know. Honestly, it might be worse for learners and early-career programmers in some ways. There is value in struggling through the old process. There is value in writing the bad code yourself, reading the error message, getting mad, walking away, coming back, and realizing the bug was exactly where you were too annoyed to look.

But the professional world is already changing.

And I am afraid I have already gone with it.

There is no going back for me.

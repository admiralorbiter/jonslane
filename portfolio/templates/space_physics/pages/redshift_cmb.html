Chapter 1

Two Sources of Redshift Important Here

Wavelength shifts can be produced by several physical mechanisms. While gravity can shift light (gravitational redshift), there are two main sources important here:

  • Radial relativistic Doppler shift: Caused by a source moving locally through space relative to an observer at velocity \(v\): \[1 + z = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} \qquad \text{where } \beta = \frac{v}{c}\] At low velocities (\(v \ll c\)), this reduces to the familiar linear approximation: \(z \approx \frac{v}{c}\).
  • Cosmological redshift: Caused by comoving emission and observation in an expanding FLRW model: \[1 + z = \frac{a(t_{\text{observed}})}{a(t_{\text{emitted}})}\]

For real astronomical observations, these different effects combine approximately multiplicatively: \[1 + z_{\text{observed}} = (1 + z_{\text{cos}})(1 + z_{\text{pec}})(1 + z_{\text{grav}})\]

⚠️ Common Misconception: Are galaxies moving through space?

In cosmological redshift, galaxies are not rushing away from us through static empty space. Successive comoving observers measure the photon's wavelength to increase as the scale factor grows during its journey.

Andromeda is currently approaching us. If the space between us is expanding, how is this possible?
Correct! Andromeda's inward peculiar velocity (\(v_{\text{pec}} \approx -120\,\text{km/s}\)) easily overcomes the local expansion rate (\(H_0 D \approx 54\,\text{km/s}\)). The Local Group is a gravitationally bound system, meaning the homogeneous, isotropic FLRW metric is not the correct local model.
Incorrect. Note that:
  • Option 1 is not the correct interpretation; the expansion rate remains positive, but is locally overwhelmed by gravity.
  • Option 3 is false; General Relativity still applies, but the homogeneous FLRW approximation is not the right model for bound structures.
Chapter 2

The Redshift Parameter z

The parameter \(z\) quantifies the amount of wavelength stretching. After correcting for local motion and other effects, cosmological redshift relates the observed and emitted scale factors through: \[a_{\text{emitted}} = \frac{a_0}{1 + z}\] With the normalization \(a_0 = 1\) (today), this simplifies to: \[a_{\text{emitted}} = \frac{1}{1 + z}\] Thus, for \(z=1\), the scale factor when light was emitted was exactly \(a = \frac{1}{2}\)—the universe was half its current size.

Because lookback time depends on the densities of matter, radiation, and dark energy, we solve the Friedmann equations dynamically to map \(z\) to physical cosmic time.

Show Mathematical Derivation for Lookback Time

To map redshift to lookback time, we integrate the expansion rate over the photon's path: \[t_{\text{lookback}}(z) = \int_0^z \frac{dz'}{(1+z') H(z')}\] where the Hubble parameter \(H(z')\) is defined in flat \(\Lambda\)CDM as: \[H(z') = H_0 \sqrt{\Omega_m(1+z')^3 + \Omega_r(1+z')^4 + \Omega_{\Lambda}}\] This makes it explicit why the conversion of redshift to time is model-dependent, relying directly on the density parameters.

⚠️ Common Misconception: Does z=1 mean velocity is c?

No. Conflating redshift directly with relative velocity via \(v = cz\) is only valid in the nearby universe (\(z \ll 1\)). A redshift of \(z=1\) simply means that light was emitted when the universe was exactly half its current size, causing its wavelength to double during transit.

Chapter 3

The Surface of Last Scattering

If we look far enough back in time, we eventually reach an era when the universe was so hot that neutral hydrogen could not exist. The entire cosmos was a dense, ionized plasma of free electrons and protons.

Photons could not travel freely because they scattered continuously off the free electrons. As the universe cooled, the fraction of free electrons fell rapidly as electrons became bound in neutral atoms. The photon mean free path increased, and radiation decoupled from matter over a finite period rather than at one perfectly sharp moment. The universe became increasingly transparent, allowing photons to begin free-streaming over cosmological distances. Drag the slider to watch this transition:

⚠️ Common Misconception: Are we seeing the Big Bang?

The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) is often called the relic of the Big Bang, but it does not let us see the origin itself. It is the boundary of our electromagnetic vision—the surface of last scattering. Prior to this, electromagnetic radiation could not propagate freely to us. Other possible messengers, such as primordial neutrinos or gravitational waves, are not subject to the same electromagnetic opacity.

Chapter 5

Standardizable Candles

To measure how the expansion rate changes over time, astronomers use standardizable candles—specifically Type Ia supernovae. These thermonuclear white dwarf explosions do not all have exactly the same raw intrinsic luminosity. However, observations of their light curves (how fast they brighten and fade) and colors allow astronomers to infer a corrected, standardized intrinsic luminosity \(L\).

By comparing the observed apparent flux \(F\) against this standardized luminosity \(L\), we calculate the luminosity distance \(D_L\): \[F = \frac{L}{4\pi D_L^2}\]

Chapter 6

The Four Pillars of Evidence

The Hot Big Bang model does not stand on a single observation. It is supported by four independent pillars of empirical evidence:

1. Cosmic Expansion

Galaxy spectra display systematic redshifts that correlate with distance, supporting large-scale cosmic expansion.

Open Issue: The Hubble tension is an unresolved discrepancy in the \(4\text{--}6\sigma\) range between local distance ladder and early-universe CMB parameters.

2. Relic CMB Radiation

The Cosmic Microwave Background is a nearly perfect blackbody spectrum, a central prediction of a hot, dense early universe and one of its strongest confirmations.

Observation: COBE's FIRAS instrument measured deviations from a perfect blackbody to be less than 50 parts per million.

3. Primordial Elements

Big Bang Nucleosynthesis successfully accounts for the primordial abundances of deuterium and Helium-4 within the cosmological model. Helium-4 comprises roughly one quarter of primordial baryonic mass.

Open Issue: Lithium-7 remains discrepant by approximately a factor of three (the Lithium problem).

4. Growth of Structure

The statistics of CMB fluctuations and the later galaxy distribution are consistent with gravitational growth from small primordial perturbations.

Inference: These variations trace primordial perturbations that later grew, through gravity, into the large-scale cosmic web.

Chapter 7

Cosmic Horizons

In an accelerating universe, there is a limit to how far light can travel to reach us. A particle horizon defines the edge of the observable universe, while an event horizon defines the limit of what we can ever influence.

⚠️ Common Misconception: Is everything observable reachable?

No. In our accelerating universe, the event horizon is currently only ~16.5 billion light-years away, while the observable universe stretches to 46.5 billion light-years. Much of the currently observable universe lies beyond the region that signals sent today could ever reach in the standard \(\Lambda\)CDM model. The exact fraction of observable galaxies is not captured by simply comparing horizon radii.

What would happen to the cosmic event horizon if dark energy disappeared and the universe became matter-dominated?
Correct! Decelerating universes have no event horizons. Without dark energy driving exponential expansion, signals sent today could eventually traverse any comoving coordinate separation.
Incorrect. Remember that the event horizon only exists because of accelerating expansion.

Chapter 3

Two Ways of Storing Energy

If we look at the bar charts on the left, we can now see two calculated quantities updating in sync with the particle's speed and position.

These are two distinct ways our defined System (Particle + Earth + Track) stores energy:

  • Kinetic Energy ($K$): The energy stored in the particle's active motion:
    $$K = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$$
  • Potential Energy ($U$): The energy stored in the configuration of the interacting system (specifically, the gravitational separation between the particle and the Earth):
    $$U = m g y$$

Observe how the Cyan bar ($K$) climbs as the particle speeds up, while the Magenta bar ($U$) drops. They act like fluid shifting between two connected cylinders.

Chapter 4

The Invariant Total

While the individual values of $K$ and $U$ oscillate wildly, their sum remains constant.

We define the Total Mechanical Energy ($E$) of our system as:

$$E_{\text{mech}} = K + U$$

Look closely at the horizontal Amber reference line on the bar chart. As the particle rolls, the tops of the $K$ and $U$ bars adjust to meet this line exactly.

Because there are no nonconservative forces (like friction) doing work on our closed system, the derivative of total mechanical energy is zero:

$$\frac{dE_{\text{mech}}}{dt} = 0$$

Chapter 5

Modifying the System & Invariance

Now let's explore two parameters: the Mass of the particle ($m$) and the Reference Zero line height ($C$).

Use the slider on the left (or click and drag the horizontal dashed line on the canvas) to change the reference height where $U = 0$.

Notice two critical things:

  1. Dragging the $U=0$ line upward subtracts an offset height $C$, causing potential energy $U$ and total energy $E$ to drop (even becoming negative if the zero reference is set above the particle).
  2. Despite these numerical shifts, the particle's actual physical motion is completely unchanged.

This is because classical forces depend only on potential energy *differences* (the slope of the curve), not absolute values:

$$F(x) = -\frac{dU}{dx} = -\frac{d}{dx}\left(U_0(x) + C\right) = -\frac{dU_0}{dx}$$

Chapter 6

Generalizing the Potentials

Energy conservation is not unique to gravity. Try toggling the presets on the left:

  • Ideal Spring: Elastic potential energy is stored in the deformation of a spring ($U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$). The force points toward the center anchor ($F = -kx$).
  • Free Space: A particle with zero interactions ($U = 0$, $F = 0$). Here, the kinetic energy is equal to total energy and remains constant indefinitely.

In all these configurations, the scalar total $E = K + U$ remains conserved, showing energy as a unified accounting language across different physical interactions.

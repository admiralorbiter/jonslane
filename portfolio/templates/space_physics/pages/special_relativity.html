Chapter 1

The Constant Speed of Light

In classical physics, velocities add together linearly. If you run at \(10\text{ mph}\) inside a train moving at \(60\text{ mph}\), an observer on the ground sees you moving at \(70\text{ mph}\).

However, Einstein's first postulate of Special Relativity states that the speed of light, \(c\), is exactly the same for all observers, regardless of their relative motion. Drag the speed dial below to accelerate Bob, then fire a light pulse. Notice that both Alice (at rest) and Bob (moving) measure the incoming photon at exactly \(1.00c\).

Concept Check:

If a star moves toward Earth at \(0.5c\) and emits a photon toward Earth, what is the speed of the photon when it reaches Earth?

Show the math ▶

Einstein's velocities are composed using the relativistic formula instead of vector addition:

$$u = \frac{u' + v}{1 + \frac{u' v}{c^2}}$$

If we set the incoming light speed \(u' = -c\) (natural units \(u' = -1\)) and the rocket velocity \(v = \beta\), we get:

$$u = \frac{-1 + \beta}{1 - \beta} = -1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad |u| = 1 = c$$

No matter what relative speed \(\beta\) you choose, the output is always \(c\).

Chapter 2

The Relativity of Simultaneity

Because the speed of light is constant for all observers, simultaneity itself fails. Two events that are simultaneous in one frame occur at different times in another frame.

In the rest frame (\(S\)), two explosions go off at the same time at positions \(x_L = -5\) and \(x_R = +5\) (orange flashes). But for Bob, riding in a spaceship moving to the right, the explosions do *not* occur simultaneously. Turn on the wavefront toggle to watch how coordinate time differs from the visual reception delay.

Concept Check:

If Bob sits exactly at the midpoint between the two explosions in his moving spaceship and subtracts the time it took light waves to propagate from each flash to his eyes, will he find that they occurred at the same time?

Show the math ▶

Simultaneity shifts are derived from the Lorentz Transformation for time:

$$t' = \gamma \left( t - \frac{v x}{c^2} \right)$$

For two events simultaneous in ground coordinates (\(\Delta t = 0\)) separated by distance \(\Delta x\):

$$\Delta t' = t'_B - t'_A = -\frac{\gamma \beta \Delta x}{c}$$

This time gap is real. Events that are simultaneous in one frame physically occur at different coordinate times in moving frames.

Chapter 3

Length Contraction

Step 1: Establish the Rest Length. When the train is at rest relative to the ruler, measuring its length is simple: align both ends with the ruler. Here, our train has a rest length of \(L_0 = 200 ext{ m}\).

Prompt: Accelerating the train will introduce length contraction. Click "Next Step" below to proceed to the measurement.

Step 2: Measure from the Platform. To measure the length of a moving train, you must record the positions of both its front and rear at the exact same time in your frame.

Set a speed below, then use the Time Scrubber to watch the train cross the detectors. Click "Take Simultaneous Measurement" to record the positions. Note the vertical flashes and the cyan/orange marks left behind.

Step 3: View from the Train. Toggle the frame view below. Keep the exact same two measurement flashes, but look at them from the train's reference frame (where the train is stationary and the tracks/detectors rush backward).

You will see that the two events are no longer simultaneous: the front detector flashed before the rear! Since they occurred at different times, they do not measure the train's length in the train frame.

Step 4: The Tunnel Paradox. Now introduce a 200 m tunnel. In the Platform frame, the train is contracted (e.g. 121.6 m) and fits inside the tunnel, allowing both doors to close simultaneously.

In the Train frame, the tunnel is contracted to 121.6 m, so the 200 m train cannot fit. Scrub through time to see how the doors close at different times (front door first, then rear door), resolving the paradox.

Concept Check:

In the train's frame (Step 3), the two measurement flashes are not simultaneous. Which flash happens first?

Show the math ▶

Length contraction is derived from setting the measurement interval \(\Delta t = 0\) in the platform frame, yielding:

$$L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = L_0 \sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$$

Applying the Lorentz transformation to the two measurement events (where the platform records both at \(t = 0\)):

$$\Delta t' = t'_B - t'_A = \gamma\left(t_B - \frac{v x_B}{c^2}\right) - \gamma\left(t_A - \frac{v x_A}{c^2}\right)$$

Since \(t_A = t_B = 0\) and \(x_B - x_A = L\), the time interval in the train frame is:

$$\Delta t' = -\frac{\gamma v L}{c^2} = -\frac{\gamma \beta L_0}{\gamma c} = -\frac{\beta L_0}{c}$$

This negative time gap means the front event occurs earlier than the rear event by \(\Delta t'\). Because the observers did not record the ends of the train simultaneously in its rest frame, the train frame sees this as an invalid measurement, resolving the apparent paradox.

Chapter 4

Time Dilation

Moving clocks run slow. Proper time (\(\tau\)), which is measured by a clock at rest relative to itself, is always shorter than the coordinate time (\(t\)) measured by stationary observers.

Click "Boost" to accelerate the rocket, then click "Play" to watch both clocks tick in real time. The moving rocket clock falls continuously behind the stationary clock on Earth.

Concept Check:

Why does the rocket clock run slower?

Show the math ▶

Time dilation is derived by evaluating the coordinate time intervals for tick events occurring at rest in the moving frame (\(\Delta x' = 0\)):

$$\Delta t = \gamma \Delta\tau = \frac{\Delta\tau}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}}$$

Because \(\gamma \ge 1\), the coordinate time elapsed on Earth is always greater than the proper time accumulated on the moving clock.

Chapter 5

Doppler Effect & Aberration

When you move toward or away from a light source, its emitted wavelength shifts. Moving toward the star causes blueshift (frequency compression); moving away causes redshift (frequency stretching).

Furthermore, at relativistic speeds, the angles of incoming light rays are warped. This relativistic aberration compresses the entire starfield ahead of you into a narrow, bright cone of light. Drag the dial to set positive (receding) or negative (approaching) velocity, and watch the star color and starfield warp.

Concept Check:

In the classical Doppler effect, observing a source at a \(90^\circ\) angle (perpendicular to motion) produces zero frequency shift. What does relativistic Doppler predict at \(90^\circ\) (Transverse Doppler)?

Show the math ▶

The relativistic Doppler equation for radial motion is:

$$\lambda_{\text{obs}} = \lambda_0 \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}}$$

Relativistic aberration updates angles of incoming light rays via:

$$\cos\theta' = \frac{\cos\theta - \beta}{1 - \beta\cos\theta}$$

As \(\beta \to 1\), \(\theta' \to 0\), compressing the star field in the forward direction.

Chapter 6

The Twin Paradox

If Bob flies away in a rocket and returns, he is younger than Alice who stayed on Earth. Why is the situation asymmetric? Bob must accelerate to turn around and return, which breaks the symmetry of the inertial frames.

Configure the speeds for the outbound leg (\(\beta_1\)) and inbound leg (\(\beta_2\)), set the Earth time duration, and launch the rocket. You can pause the simulation at turnaround to inspect how Bob's plane of simultaneity sweeps across Earth's timeline.

Concept Check:

Does the physical acceleration force at turnaround cause Bob to age slower?

Show the math ▶

The proper time accumulated along Bob's path is calculated using the Minkowski metric integral:

$$\tau = \int_0^T \sqrt{1 - \beta(t)^2}\, dt$$

For symmetric outbound and return velocities \(\beta\), this resolves simply to:

$$\tau_{\text{Bob}} = T \sqrt{1 - \beta^2} = \frac{T}{\gamma} < T = \tau_{\text{Alice}}$$

Bob's path is a bent curve in spacetime, which is geometrically shorter than Alice's straight path.

Concept Check:

If two events have a spacelike separation (\(s^2 < 0\)), what does this mean causally?

Show the math ▶

The spacetime interval $s^2$ between two events is defined as:

$$s^2 = c^2 \Delta t^2 - \Delta x^2$$

Applying a Lorentz boost rotates the coordinates hyperbolically. The coordinates transform, but the metric interval is conserved: $s^2 = s'^2$.

Three separation domains exist:

  • Timelike (\(s^2 > 0\)): Causal connection possible. A frame exists where they occur at the same place.
  • Lightlike (\(s^2 = 0\)): Connectable only by light.
  • Spacelike (\(s^2 < 0\)): No connection possible. A frame exists where they occur simultaneously.
Chapter 8

The Cosmic Speed Limit

Why can't you accelerate past the speed of light? It is not because of a mechanical block, but because the energy required to accelerate further diverges to infinity as \(v \to c\).

Click and hold "Apply Thrust" to apply constant proper acceleration (\(1g\)). Watch the graphs below: coordinate velocity asymptotes to \(1.00c\), coordinate acceleration plummets to zero, and kinetic energy explodes exponentially.

Concept Check:

Why does coordinate acceleration drop to zero even though proper engine thrust remains constant?

Show the math ▶

Applying constant proper acceleration $\alpha$ results in a coordinate velocity profile of:

$$v(t) = \frac{\alpha t}{\sqrt{1 + \left( \frac{\alpha t}{c} \right)^2}}$$

As $t \to \infty$, $v(t) \to c$, showing that the velocity asymptotes but never crosses $c$. The coordinate acceleration falls as:

$$a(t) = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma^3}$$

Kinetic energy diverges exponentially:

$$K = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$$

Because \(\gamma \to \infty\) as \(v \to c\), the energy cost of accelerating further becomes infinite.

What Does It Mean for the Universe to Expand?

What quantity changes when we say the universe is expanding? Instead of cataloging events, let's explore the mathematical model describing how cosmological distances change over time.

Chapter 1

The Wrong Picture

When thinking of the Big Bang, we often picture a tiny firecracker expanding into empty surrounding space. This is the explosion metaphor: a localized object with a defined center and an outer boundary, bursting into pre-existing emptiness.

But modern cosmology is based on a completely different premise: the expansion of space itself. Note: "Expanding space" is a description of how cosmological distances evolve under a changing metric, not a claim that space behaves like a physical stretching fabric or material substance.

Chapter 3

Two Kinds of Coordinates

To model an expanding system mathematically, we split distance measurements into two distinct categories:

  • Comoving Coordinate (\(\chi\)): A coordinate grid that is locked to the markers. As space expands, the comoving coordinates of galaxies do not change.
  • Proper Distance (\(D\)): The spatial distance between two locations measured along a surface of constant cosmic time. This can be imagined as an idealized chain of local rulers laid between the locations at that cosmic instant.

They are linked by the scale factor \(a(t)\): $$D(t) = a(t) \cdot \chi$$

⚠️ Common Misconception: Does a physical rod expand?

If space expands, why don't our rulers, solar systems, or galaxies expand alongside it? The scale factor describes the large-scale separation of comoving, unbound regions. Bound systems must be modeled using their own local dynamics. Within atoms, solids, solar systems, and galaxies, local electromagnetic or gravitational interactions dominate, so their internal distances do not simply scale with \(a(t)\). Expansion is not a physical "drag" force pulling objects apart.

Chapter 4

The Scale Factor

The parameter \(a(t)\) represents the relative size scale of the universe. Because its absolute scale is arbitrary, cosmologists normalize it to \(1\) at the present day: $$a(t_0) = 1$$

How \(a(t)\) changes over time depends on what components populate the universe. In a simplified model, we look at three extreme cases:

  • Radiation Dominated (Spatially Flat): Early universe, expanding as \(a(t) \propto t^{1/2}\) (decelerating).
  • Matter Dominated (Spatially Flat): Middle era, expanding as \(a(t) \propto t^{2/3}\) (decelerating).
  • Cosmological Constant Dominated (Spatially Flat): Late era, expanding exponentially as \(a(t) \propto e^{H_\Lambda t}\) (accelerating).
Model Assumptions / Law
Radiation Flat, radiation only: \(a(t) \propto t^{1/2}\) (decelerating)
Matter Flat, pressureless matter only: \(a(t) \propto t^{2/3}\) (decelerating)
Cosmological Constant Flat, constant vacuum energy density (\(w = -1\)): \(a(t) \propto e^{H_\Lambda t}\) (accelerating)

Note: These are simplified single-component model universes designed to show how different energy forms affect distance scaling, not consecutive parts of one real universe history.

Chapter 5

Expansion as a Rate

To measure how fast proper distance changes, we differentiate \(D(t) = a(t)\chi\) with respect to time: $$\dot{D}(t) = \dot{a}(t)\chi$$

Since \(\chi = D(t) / a(t)\), we can substitute it back: $$\dot{D}(t) = \frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)} D(t)$$

We define the Hubble Parameter \(H(t)\) as the fractional expansion rate: $$H(t) \equiv \frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}$$

This gives Hubble's Law: the Hubble recession rate of a comoving galaxy is directly proportional to its proper distance: $$v_{\text{rec}} = H(t) D(t)$$

Let's look at the crucial distinction between these parameters. Drag the time slider \(t\) to inspect a universe that transitions from decelerating (matter-dominated) to accelerating (dark-energy-dominated) according to: $$a(t) = t^{2/3} + 0.05 t^2$$

💡 Insight: How can the Hubble parameter decrease if expansion is speeding up?

Look at the graphs above between \(t = 1.0\) and \(t = 4.0\). The acceleration \(\ddot{a}\) becomes positive and the absolute speed \(\dot{a}\) increases. Proper distances between comoving locations are accelerating apart because \(\ddot{a} > 0\). Yet the Hubble parameter \(H = \dot{a}/a\) is decreasing. Why? Because the size of the universe \(a\) is growing faster than the expansion speed \(\dot{a}\). The fractional rate of growth decreases even while the absolute rate of growth increases!

Chapter 6

No Preferred Center

Hubble's law, \(v = H D\), is not a proof that we are at the center of the universe. Because the relationship is strictly linear, every comoving observer sees a Hubble diagram centered on themselves. Note: A linear velocity-distance law alone does not distinguish cosmological expansion from a localized explosion. The physical distinction lies in general relativity, where expansion is a metric property of a homogeneous spacetime without a central explosion point or an external empty space.

Chapter 7

Hubble Flow vs. Peculiar Motion

In the real universe, galaxies are not strictly anchored to comoving coordinates. They also move locally due to the gravity of neighboring mass structures.

A galaxy's observed velocity is the vector sum of two components: $$v_{\text{observed}} = v_{\text{Hubble}} + v_{\text{peculiar}}$$ Note: This simple additive decomposition is an approximation valid for nearby systems at non-relativistic speeds. At cosmological distances, relative velocities must be computed using general relativistic geodesics and light travel times.

  • Hubble Flow (\(v_{\text{Hubble}} = H \cdot D\)): The expansion of space carrying the galaxy away.
  • Peculiar Motion (\(v_{\text{peculiar}}\)): The galaxy's local motion through space.

Chapter 8

Model Scope & Limitations

Every cosmological claim is only as solid as the model it is built on. Here is how our kinematics model stands in terms of evidence and scientific consensus:

✅ Well Established Within This Framework
  • Large-scale Hubble expansion: Directly measured by redshifts of distant galaxies.
  • Homogeneity and Isotropy: The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) shows the early universe was uniform to 1 part in 100,000.
  • Decoupling of Bound Systems: Gravitationally bound systems (atoms, planets, solar systems, galaxies) do not expand.
⚙️ Model-Dependent Assumptions
  • General Relativity: We assume spacetime curvature behaves according to Einstein's equations.
  • Dark Energy Profile: Current calculations assume dark energy is a constant vacuum density (\(w = -1\)). If dark energy evolves, future predictions change.
  • Curvature: The universe is extremely close to flat (\(\Omega_k \approx 0\)), but a tiny curvature would alter the global geometry.
❓ Genuinely Open Questions
  • What is Dark Energy? Its acceleration is well-measured, but its physical origin is unknown.
  • The Hubble Tension: Measurements of \(H_0\) using the CMB conflict with measurements using nearby supernova ladders, hinting at missing physics.
  • Initial Conditions: Why was the early universe in such a low-entropy, smooth state?

❓ Questions This Model Does Not Yet Answer

Our current model is kinematic—it describes the geometry of expansion but doesn't explain the underlying dynamics. In future modules, we will address:

  • What determines the scale factor \(a(t)\)? (Friedmann's dynamical equations and the density of matter, radiation, and dark energy).
  • Is the universe spatially finite or infinite? (Global topology of flat, open, or closed spaces).
  • Why did expansion transition from deceleration to acceleration? (Dark energy density vs. diluting matter density).
  • How do we measure \(a(t)\) from ancient light? (Cosmological redshift and standard candles like Type Ia supernovae).
  • What is the physical origin of dark energy? (Cosmological constant \(\Lambda\) vs. evolving fields).

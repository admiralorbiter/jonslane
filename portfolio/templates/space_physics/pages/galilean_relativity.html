Galilean Relativity: Whose State?

If two observers are moving relative to one another, who measures the "true" state of the universe?

Chapter 1

Who's Watching?

In classical physics, there is no absolute "center" to space. To define the position of a particle, we must first choose a reference frame—a set of coordinates anchored to an observer.

Imagine a stationary observer, \(S\) (cyan), standing on the ground, and a second observer, \(S'\) (magenta), riding in a train car moving at a relative speed \(u\).

Chapter 2

The Ball in the Train

What happens when we perform an experiment inside the train? Let's throw a ball straight up.

From the perspective of the passenger inside the train (\(S'\)), the ball simply goes straight up and falls straight back down. It travels in a one-dimensional vertical line.

But to an observer standing on the ground (\(S\)), the ball is moving horizontally with the train while it rises and falls. To the ground observer, the ball traces out a two-dimensional parabolic arc!

Chapter 3

How the State Vector Transforms

We can write down exactly how the coordinates shift using the Galilean coordinate transformations. If the moving frame travels along the x-axis at speed \(u\), the position \(x'\), velocity \(v'\), and acceleration \(a'\) relate to the ground measurements as:

\[x' = x - ut, \quad v' = v - u, \quad a' = a\]

Observe how the numbers shift by scrubbing the relative frame speed \(u = \) 10 m/s:

Let a particle have position \(x = 50\,\text{m}\), velocity \(v = 25\,\text{m/s}\), and acceleration \(a = 3.5\,\text{m/s}^2\). In the moving frame \(S'\), its state transforms to:
Position: \(x' = 50 - (\text{u} \times t)\) → 50.0 m (at \(t = 1\,\text{s}\))
Velocity: \(v' = 25 - u\) → 15.0 m/s
Acceleration: \(a' = a\) → 3.5 m/s² (completely invariant!)

Symmetry & Observer Equivalence

Because acceleration is identical in both frames (\(a' = a\)), Newton's laws of motion hold equally in both. A passenger on the train and a person on the ground will discover the exact same laws of physics!

Chapter 6

The Limit: Where Galileo Breaks Down

Galilean relativity works perfectly for things we see in everyday life. But what happens when objects move close to the speed of light \(c\)?

In Galilean physics, if you run forward at \(20\,\text{m/s}\) inside a train moving at \(90\,\text{m/s}\), your speed relative to the ground is simply \(20 + 90 = 110\,\text{m/s}\). Under this rule, if you shine a flashlight, the light should travel at \(c + u\)—exceeding the universal speed limit!

To resolve this, Einstein formulated Special Relativity. Galilean velocity transformations are only an approximation. The true relativistic velocity addition formula is:

\[v_{\text{ground}} = \frac{v' + u}{1 + \frac{u v'}{c^2}}\]

Classical Mechanics & State Vectors

Why Position Is Not Enough

What information must we know about a physical system right now in order to predict what it will do next?

Chapter 1

A Particle Exists

Imagine the simplest possible physical system: a single point particle confined to move along a straight, one-dimensional track.

At any given moment, the particle is sitting at some coordinate relative to the origin. This value is its position (\(x\)).

Chapter 2

But Position Alone is Not Enough

Suppose we want to predict the future. If we only know that our particle is sitting at position \(x = 0\) right now, do we know where it will be one second from now?

No. It could sit still. It could drift to the right. It could zip to the left.

To illustrate this fundamental limitation, look at the two particles below. They start at the exact same position, yet their paths instantly diverge. One moves left, while the other moves right.

Same starting coordinate, completely different futures. To predict what happens next, we need another piece of information.

Chapter 3

Velocity Completes the State

The missing piece is the rate of change of position over time, which we call velocity (\(v = dx/dt\)).

Together, position and velocity form the State Vector of the particle: $$\mathbf{S}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x(t) \\ v(t) \end{bmatrix}$$

If you know both numbers, you know the complete "state" of the system at this moment.

Try dragging the particle below to set the initial position \(x_0\) = 50 m, and drag the arrowhead tip to set the initial velocity \(v_0\) = 12 m/s. Observe how the slope of the position line changes on the graph below it.

Chapter 4

Evolution Laws: What Changes the State?

Position changes because of velocity. But what changes velocity? That requires acceleration (\(a = dv/dt\)), which is caused by forces: \(F = ma\).

According to Newton, we can describe the movement of any physical system as a set of coupled differential equations:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = v, \quad \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{F(x,v,t)}{m}$$

By changing the force law \(F\), we change how the velocity evolves. Select different Force Laws below to watch how the particle accelerates and decelerates under different physical rules:

Chapter 5

Phase Space: State as a Point

Instead of tracking position and velocity separately over time, we can plot them directly against each other on a 2D coordinate grid called Phase Space.

At any instant, the complete state of the particle is represented by a single dot \((x, v)\). As the particle moves, this dot traces out a path.

Select a force law below to watch the orbit unfold. Under a Spring Force, the state vector traces a closed circle (energy conservation). Under Viscous Drag, it spirals inward as energy decays.

Chapter 6

Different Solvers, Different Futures

In the real world, computers cannot solve differential equations exactly. They approximate the next step using a time step size: \(\Delta t\).

The choice of algorithm is critical. Forward Euler (magenta) is simple, but it is unstable—it continuously artificially adds energy to the spring, causing it to spiral outward. Euler-Cromer (cyan) is a symplectic solver that preserves phase space volume and oscillates stably.

Adjust the simulation step size \(\Delta t\) = 0.10 seconds. Observe what happens when \(\Delta t\) crosses the critical threshold: \(\Delta t_{\text{crit}} = 2.00\) s.

⚠️ Numerical Instability Triggered! The selected time step \(\Delta t \ge 2.0\) s exceeds the stability limit. Both solvers explode.

Chapter 7

Galilean Relativity: Whose State?

We have looked at the state vector from a single perspective. But what if we look at the same particle from a coordinate frame S' moving at a relative speed \(u\)?

Under the Galilean transformation, coordinates shift: $$x' = x - ut, \quad v' = v - u, \quad a' = a$$

Adjust the observer's relative speed \(u\) = 3 m/s. Observe the tracks below. Position and velocity are different for each frame, but the **acceleration graph** matches exactly. The laws of physics are invariant!
